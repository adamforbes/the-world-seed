Core Ruleset

The World Seed

A collaborative worldbuilding ruleset for roleplaying in any rule system, based on The Quiet Year and Once Upon a Time by Michael Patashnik.

Introduction

While there are many, many components to a successful roleplaying campaign, the very best campaigns begin with a deep and varied world in which to set the story. Stories told in these worlds grip us because we feel a part of them. When we truly feel a part of the setting we traverse, we are no longer simple game players rolling dice and tallying numbers. We become more, a very part of that world. Every round of battle rests on a blade's edge as the fate of this shared narrative hangs in the balance. The people, places, and things are no longer simply a backdrop, it is our world; what a delight it is to adventure within!

The Traditional Method

In most roleplaying games, the DM builds the world. Before the first session they draw the maps, write the histories and name the gods. It's a generous gift, and often a beautiful one.

But a world that arrives finished is hard to step into. Players meet it as pages to read, and pages are easy to skim. It becomes a place they visit rather than one they belong to, and the DM is left carrying the work of making it feel real.

A New Proposal

This guide offers another way: build the world together. In a session 0, before the adventure begins, everyone at the table takes turns shaping it, from the birth of its first Gods to the founding of its Nations. Nobody needs to read up on the world, because they were there when it was made.

From then on, the work of making the world feel real is shared. The DM becomes less its sole author and more its steward: they can still change it as the campaign needs, but should honor what everyone built together.

Players come to the first session already at home in the world. They know its history, geography and beliefs as well as the DM does, and they play their characters as people who belong there, not visitors.

We've played in campaigns built this way, and the difference is night and day.

Beyond The Quiet Year

Rewrite this section. It still compares only the original Once Upon a Time to The Quiet Year. Say how The World Seed differs from both.

This guide is heavily based on The Quiet Year, an excellent worldbuilding game in its own right. In The Quiet Year, players take turns narrating an ever-expanding world, filled with conflict, belief and discovery. In broad strokes, The World Seed is the same. Together with your fellow players you will flesh out the world, raising mountains and carving seas as nations rise and fall. The specific mechanics of The World Seed are also similar to The Quiet Year.

So how do the two differ? The Quiet Year is designed to be a complete storytelling experience, freely mixing world-definition with the thoughts and conversations of individual members of a single society. In contrast The World Seed knows itself to be only the preamble to the true story, the campaign. This allows us to focus on broad and sweeping worldbuilding efforts like creating continents and naming gods while putting off more narrow concerns like individual city politics until the campaign begins.

Further, The Quiet Year nominally takes place over the scope of a single year. This limits and narrows its scope, allowing a story to take place. In contrast, The World Seed takes place over four distinct Eras, each stretching for many centuries or even millennia.

Finally, The World Seed suggests specifically tracking named Entities that are created as part of playing the game such as Gods, Peoples, and Nations. Doing so gives the DM natural paths of extension, allowing them to more fully flesh out the jointly-created world while remaining true to its initial conception.

The Four Eras

The World Seed takes place over Four Eras. These Eras represent the ages of the world and recorded history. Throughout each Era, Peoples rise and fall, Actors accomplish great deeds, and even the world is irrecoverably changed as the very land itself shifts underfoot.

Past and Present

The first three Eras of The World Seed take place in the past; no one alive (or at least very, very few) will have personally lived through these Eras. The final Era of The World Seed is the current Era and the chronological setting of the campaign.

While the Eras occur in order, they should not be interpreted as having a strictly even-paced timeline. Years may ebb and flow within an Era and there may be an unspecified amount of time between Eras.

Fact and Fiction

The contents of all Eras within The World Seed should not be interpreted as authoritative and complete records. Rather, these are tales and knowledge that have made it to public recognition in the modern Era. As the campaign progresses characters may discover new truths, ones that clarify, challenge, or even contradict the histories they knew. For the three Eras in the past, the truth may have been lost to time or warped by biased actors. For the final Era in the present, the truth may yet be hidden, not yet known by the characters or the world at large.

Era of Myth

The first Era is the Era of Myth, a time of mystical uncertainty and infinite possibility. This ancient historical Era details the origins of the world we find ourselves in. Peoples and Gods take their first tentative steps to explore this new and strange world they find themselves in. Technology and civilization are limited, while magic is instinctive and primal. While there may have been many named heroes and villains, a very select few are still remembered today. Those that are are remembered as the very first champions who accomplished stupendous feats of strength and will.

Era of Antiquity

The second Era is the Era of Antiquity, a time of lofty idealism and burgeoning progress. In this Era, civilization expands at a rapid rate. Peoples organize themselves into cities and cities into Nations. The first grand wars are fought under brightly colored banners for higher ideals. Gods are still present but now may intervene less actively and less destructively. Economic systems begin to blossom, funneling resources into all kinds of progress. Leveraging these systems, individual Actors may attempt world-shattering feats such as contacting another plane or briefly stopping the flow of time.

Era of Ruin

The third Era is the Era of Ruin, a time of decaying promise and infectious distrust. The rampant growth of the Era of Antiquity is still present, but malicious actors begin to appear to prey upon the shared progress. Nations collapse under the weight of their corrupt leaders as despotic empires spread their dominion with an iron fist. Wars are no longer fought between respected equals, but now between good and evil, with each side believing its victory to be critical to preserving civilization as they know it. Actors undertake ever more dangerous quests, some failing with dire consequences.

While this is the Era immediately before the quest, large portions of the history of this Era may yet be forgotten: Every library burned in the conquest of a city robs the modern world of a piece of its past.

Era of Promise

The fourth and final Era is the Era of Promise, a time of hard-fought rebirth and discovery anew. As the world reconciles with its immediate past, adventurers plunder the relics of the past to rediscover history lost. The world is at a crossroads: Can a higher ideal again be realized? Or is the relief from the darkness only a brief respite, doomed to end all too soon?

It is in this Era that our adventure takes place. This Era will end at a randomized time, signaling the end of The World Seed and the beginning of the campaign.

Entities

Throughout The World Seed, you will be asked to create and keep track of a number of Entities. These are named people, places, and things that form the bedrock of the story.

Consider keeping track of Entities using a stack of index cards or post-it notes and a pencil. Whenever you are asked to create a new Entity, write the name (and any other information) on the card. If you are asked to make a change to the Entity, make sure to do so such that the original information is not lost.

Types of Entities

Types of Entities in The World Seed: Places, Gods, Peoples, Actors, and Nations.

Places

Places are significant, named locations in the world, encompassing both natural wonders and artificial constructions. They are the settings upon which the story unfolds.

A Place can be:

  • A natural Place: A part of the land, sea, or sky. This may be a pre-existing biome that is only now discovered by a People. Alternatively, it could be a modification to an existing natural Place, creating a newly named location within a pre-existing area (e.g. The Serpent's Spine Mountains, The Whispering Fen, The Azure Ocean).
  • A constructed Place: An artificial construction created by an Entity. These notable pieces of architecture are typically the result of a Journey (e.g. The Library of Rhean, The Adamantine Fortress, The Sky-Piercing Beacon).

Places can be of any size, from as small as a specific tree to as large as an entire ocean or a moon. So long as it can be ascribed a single name, it is a valid Place.

Places may be entirely mundane or inherently magical. If the latter, the sky's the limit! Do not feel the need to explain why or how the Place is magical; this may be as much a mystery to the modern world as it is to you!

Gods

The playwrights behind the stage, the Gods are powerful Entities that leave lasting mark on the world. Regardless of the exact rules of divinity in the world, it is generally useful to start with the following basic guidelines.

Gods have a particular domain or domains. These are areas that they are particularly connected to and may be particularly powerful within. This may be as physical as "Fire" to as idealistic as "Truth."

Gods are exceptionally powerful but not omnipotent; there may be deeds beyond their abilities they must employ Actors to fulfill.

Gods have varying levels of knowledge but are rarely omniscient. They have some level of immortality, at least in terms of not aging, but may be vulnerable to attack.

Peoples

Before civilizations and notable individuals, the world is populated by a number of Peoples. While these may begin with the Peoples your game already offers (e.g. Elves, Dwarves), further Peoples may split off and deviate from their parent branch. For example, a sect of Elves may move underground and, over the millennia, become Drow.

When Naming a new People, no explanation is needed as to where or how they come into being. They simply appear on the stage of the story.

Any People your game already has is fair game, and so is one easily adapted from it: "Hellspawn" can simply be Tieflings. If a People has no clear match in your game, get your DM's permission before introducing them.

When branching a People, consider explaining which People they branched from, and how and why. What divides those who stayed with the existing cultural branch from those who took a new direction?

Once established, a People will likely spread across the world, living in many lands while sharing a common culture. Mark those lands on the board in broad strokes. They needn't be exact, but they show who lives within each Nation's borders.

Actors

The central figures who exert influence on history are known as Actors.

An Actor can be:

  • An Individual: A single noteworthy person, such as a hero, a villain, or a powerful mage.
  • A Group: A collection of individuals acting as one, such as a pair of siblings, a council of elders, or a thieves' guild.
  • A Creature: A significant non-humanoid Entity, such as a great beast, a powerful monster, or a sentient construct.

When you first define an Actor, you must also consider their loyalties. Do they owe allegiance to a particular God, Nation, or ideal? Are they a recluse, acting only for themselves? Or are they part of a collective, driven by the goals of their People? An Actor's loyalties are the foundation of their motivations.

By default, all Actors are mortal; individuals and creatures may only become immortal through extraordinary events or arduous Journeys.

Nations

Nations arise when Peoples bind themselves under a shared rule: a king, a council, a law, a khan. While some early Nations are ethnic enclaves of a single People, later ones are more likely to be founded upon ideals and systems, populated by a diverse citizenry.

Even more so than a People, Nations have and project a belief system. When Naming a Nation, consider what type of government it will use. Do all citizens have equal standing? Is a particular faith or belief system paramount? Of course, all of these may change over time.

Draw a Nation's borders on the board when you Name it, and redraw them as they change. Unless something says otherwise, the Peoples who live in a Nation are those whose lands lie within its borders, whether that's one People or many. Borders are porous and Peoples move, so this shifts with the story. Note only what's unusual, with a Quality or Relationship: a Nation that welcomes Peoples from far away (Multicultural), one that closes itself to outsiders (Insular), or one that has driven a People out (Exiled [People]).

A Nation includes any number of settlements: villages, towns, cities, camps, and seasonal grounds. Only the notable ones need tracking: Name a capital or other important settlement as a Place within the Nation's borders.

Qualities & Relationships

Throughout The World Seed, you may be asked to assign an Entity a Quality. A Quality is a short adjective describing the overall behavior of the Entity in question (e.g. a Place may be Lush, a People may be Honorable, a Nation may be Austere).

A Relationship contains the Entity name and a short description of the relationship between the two (e.g. An Actor may be Daughter of [Actor], a God might have Despises [God], a Nation may be Keepers of [Place]). When adding a Relationship to one Entity, you may add as many related Relationships to other Entities.

Qualities and Relationships are not permanent: An event may cause an Entity to change its Qualities or Relationships. For example, a magical winter may rob a rainforest of its ecosystem, removing its Lush Quality. That said, when handling Qualities and Relationships, make sure to keep a record. Even if a Quality or Relationship is lost, history still remembers that the Entity once had it. (The Rainforest is remembered as once being Lush, even if it is no longer.)

Many effects may request that you give an Entity a positive or negative Quality or Relationship. What qualifies as each is entirely up to your jurisdiction.

The Game

Now that we have laid out all of the mechanics you will interact with, it's time to play The World Seed! The World Seed can be played with any number of players but is best with three to eight. The game takes approximately five hours to play to completion but can be split by Era to allow play over separate chunks of time.

Preparing the Game

Game Components
  • A large, markable surface (e.g. whiteboard) and colored markers for each player.
  • Index cards or post-it notes to track Entities.
  • A standard 52-card deck of playing cards, plus two Jokers.
Player Setup
  • Seed the World: Each player prepares three starting Entities: a continent (Place), a God, and a People, each with one Quality.
Era Deck Preparation
  • Create Era Decks: Separate the cards by suit to create four decks: Myth (♥), Antiquity (♦), Ruin (♣), and Promise (♠).
  • Refine Decks: Remove one random card from each deck face-down. (If you remove the Ace of Spades, return it and draw another).
  • Adjust for Player Count:
    • 5 Players: Add the Red Joker to Antiquity (♦) and Black Joker to Ruin (♣).
    • 7 Players: Add the Red Joker to Myth (♥).
  • Finalize Decks: Shuffle each deck.

The Game Board

Throughout the game, players will perform actions, Name Entities, and Narrate Events. Whenever a player does any of these, they draw on the board a depiction of their change. At the end of the game, the resulting tapestry is four Eras of history in pictorial form.

Generally, nothing is ever erased from the board. If an effect tells you to remove an Entity from the board, draw its removal on the board.

Playing The Game

The game consists of the four named Eras, in order. Within each Era, perform the following steps:

  • Start the Era: Players add new Entities to the game according to Starting an Era.
  • Playing the Era: Players take turns in a clockwise circle around the table as described in Turn Mechanics. Continue until the Era deck is empty. In the next Era, play continues with the next player in turn order.
  • End the Era: Players resolve Journeys, leave Legacies, and remove Actors according to Ending an Era.

Defining the Scope

Before play begins, have a brief "Session 0" conversation to set expectations for the world you intend to play in. Are you crafting a high fantasy epic, a gritty sci-fi universe, or something else entirely? Discussing the end goal now—deciding if there are guns, the prevalence of magic, or world's base biome—provides a shared sense of direction and cohesion. Because even the smallest choices made in the Era of Myth ripple forward through the ages, this alignment ensures the history you build together leads toward a satisfying destination.

Starting an Era

At the start of each Era, players seed the world with some Entities. Players add Entities in turn order, starting with the player who will take the first turn within the Era.

Era of Myth: Players add their prepared Place, God, and People Entities with one Quality each to the game. Draw the continent Places on the board, taking up appropriate space. Each player adds one Relationship between their God and another God. No Actors are added at the start of the Era of Myth.

Every other Era: Each player adds an Actor with one Quality and one Relationship to the game.

The dawn of an Era is a canvas of possibility. Seize this moment to Name the Actors who will shape the age, and set them upon their grand Journeys. The seeds of their stories, planted now, will blossom into the legends of tomorrow.

Starting an Era doesn't give out any Journeys; the passage above is an invitation, not a rule. Taking a Journey action early in the Era is a great way to set your new Actor on their path.

Turn Mechanics

On your turn, do the following:

  1. Take an Action: Choose between Naming, Journey, or Narration.
  2. Draw an Era Card: Read its text aloud from the Era Cards page and follow its instructions.
  3. Bonus Naming (Optional): Name a new Entity of one of the current Era's Bonus types. You get one per turn, and you choose when to take it, as long as it's before you roll for Journeys.
  4. Roll for Journeys: Every player rolls a d20 for each of their Journeys in progress, except any given this turn. See Journey.

Action

Each turn, you must take one action: Naming, Journey, or Narration. These options are detailed below.

Naming

You Name a new Entity into the game. The following Entities can be Named in their listed Eras below.

As part of the Naming, describe the Entity in one to two sentences, then add the additional information listed for its type under Types of Entities and give the Entity one Quality. Add any Relationships to this Entity that make sense.

Naming Options

The types of Entities you can Name evolve with each Era. Early Eras focus on establishing the world's foundational elements (Gods, Peoples, Places), while later Eras emphasize the actions of Actors and the rise of Nations.

Journey

You give an existing Entity an undertaking to accomplish. A Journey may be anything, provided it takes a nontrivial amount of time to accomplish and that it has a chance of failure. For example:

  • A People, driven by prophecy, seek a new home.
  • An Actor is given a quest to slay a great beast.
  • A Nation begins construction of a great wall to divide the realm.

When you give a Journey, declare what is being undertaken, then choose a Journey size from the table below.

When the remaining tasks reach zero, the Journey completes. You decide if a Journey ends in success or failure unless otherwise noted. If successful, at least half of the outcomes must be positive. If failure, at least half must be negative. You can choose the same option more than once.

When a Journey completes, choose from these outcomes:

  • Add or remove a single Quality to a related Entity.
  • Add or remove a Relationship between that Entity and another related Entity.
  • Add or remove a task to/from another Journey (Costs 2 outcomes).
  • Name or remove a single Entity (Costs 2 outcomes).

If two Journeys complete on the same turn, the one that was given first completes first.

Narration

You Narrate an event that occurs in the world. An event may be anything, provided it occurs over a short period of time and is something that "happened" rather than a goal that was worked towards. When Narrating, include what happens and who it affects.

In response to hearing the Narration, each other player in turn order starting with the next player and ending with you may select one affected Entity. If they do, they perform one of the following, relating to the Narration:

  • Add or remove a single Quality to that Entity.
  • Add or remove a Relationship between that Entity and another related Entity (e.g. Worships [God], Enemy of [Nation], Rival of [Actor]).
  • Increase or decrease a related Journey's remaining task count by 1.
  • Name or remove a single Entity (only if all players agree).

Once a player selects an affected Entity for a Narration, further players cannot select the same Entity. If a player has nothing further to add for a given Narration, they can pass.

While Narration is key to binding the world together, it can also take much longer than other actions. Players responding to Narration should try to keep their responses short to keep the game moving!

Draw a Card

After taking your action, draw an Era Card. Draw the top card of the Era deck and reveal it, then follow the instructions.

Bonus Naming

In each Era, some types of Entities appear more often than others. These are that Era's Bonus types, marked Bonus in its row of the Naming Options table.

Once per turn, you may Name a new Entity of one of the current Era's Bonus types. Name it the same way as the Naming action. Bonus Naming is optional and comes on top of your action, never instead of it. You choose when to take it: before your action, after it, or after you draw an Era Card, as long as it's before you roll for Journeys. Your action can still be a Naming, even of the same type.

Ending an Era

An Era ends when its deck runs out. Between it and the next Era, an undefined amount of time passes. At the end of every Era but the last, do the following, in order:

  1. Complete Journeys: All Journeys in progress complete, starting with the one with the fewest tasks remaining. A Journey with 3 or fewer tasks remaining completes normally; one with 4 or more completes in failure.
  2. Leave Legacies: Each player may select one departing Actor to leave their mark on the world. See Legacy.
  3. Remove Actors: All Actors die, unless specified otherwise. Keep their Entity cards, but remove them from play. They may be referenced or even changed later: even though these Actors are no longer alive, knowledge of them is not lost.

Legacy

Each player may select a single departing Actor to leave their mark on the world. Name a new Entity or rename an existing one so that it carries the Actor's memory (e.g. a city named for a hero, a tyrant's tomb, a People who keep a prophet's teachings).

The marked Entity gains a Relationship to the Actor (e.g. Named for [Actor], Tomb of [Actor], Keepers of [Actor]). If it is a new Entity, its Quality is one of the Actor's Qualities.
